Berkeley graduate admissions: two departments

Source: R/data-berk_sub.R
berk_sub.Rd

Admission decisions for the 1,169 applicants to two departments of the graduate school of the University of California, Berkeley, in fall 1973, one row per applicant. The two departments are B and E of the admissions table in base R's datasets::UCBAdmissions, which identifies departments only by letter. With three binary variables, the data are a compact example of confounding and Simpson's paradox in logistic regression.

Usage

berk_sub

Format

A tibble with 1,169 rows and 3 variables.

admitted

Admitted. Values: 0 = rejected; 1 = admitted. Type: integer. One row per applicant, expanded from the published admissions table, so many rows are identical.

male

Male applicant. Values: 0 = female; 1 = male. Type: integer.

engineering

Department: B (1) or E (0). Values: 0 = department E; 1 = department B. Type: integer. Departments B and E of datasets::UCBAdmissions (berkeley codes 1 and 4). The names engineering and psychology are guesses from the teaching materials the data came from; the real departments are anonymous.

See codebook("berk_sub") for this table as a data frame.

Source

Bickel, P. J., Hammel, E. A., & O'Connell, J. W. (1975). Sex bias in graduate admissions: Data from Berkeley. Science, 187(4175), 398-404. doi:10.1126/science.187.4175.398

Subset of berkeley prepared for the UC Berkeley EDUC 275B course. Provenance: verified.

Details

Departments. engineering = 1 is department B (585 applicants) and engineering = 0 is department E (584 applicants); they are departments 1 and 4 of berkeley. The names "engineering" (for B) and "psychology" (for E) are guesses, not documented facts: the real departments are anonymous. The variable keeps its old name, but describe the departments as B and E.

One row per applicant. The rows were expanded from a table of counts, so there are only eight distinct rows (2 departments x 2 genders x 2 decisions). A logistic regression on the 1,169 rows gives the same estimates and standard errors as a binomial regression on the four department-by-gender counts.

Coding. male is 1 for men and 0 for women, whereas berkeley codes female (1 = women). No value is missing, and the values are unchanged in version 1.0.0.

Simpson's paradox. Overall, 54% of men and 27% of women were admitted (odds ratio for men 3.25). But 96% of department B's applicants were men, against 33% of department E's, and B admitted 63% of its applicants against 25% for E. Controlling for department, the odds ratio for men falls to about 1.1 and is not statistically significant; in department B, women were admitted at a slightly higher rate than men.

Suggested analyses.

  • Logistic regression of admitted on male, without and with engineering: confounding and omitted-variable bias.

  • The interaction male * engineering: does the gender difference differ between the two departments?

  • The same models fitted to the aggregated counts with a binomial response, cbind(admitted, rejected), to compare individual and grouped data.

See also

berkeley for five departments (B to F) of the same admissions data; datasets::UCBAdmissions in base R for the full six-department table of counts; codebook() and list_datasets().

Examples

head(berk_sub)
#> # A tibble: 6 × 3
#>   admitted  male engineering
#>      <int> <int>       <int>
#> 1        0     1           1
#> 2        0     0           1
#> 3        0     1           0
#> 4        0     0           0
#> 5        1     1           1
#> 6        1     0           1

# Share of men and admission rate by department (engineering = 1 is B)
aggregate(cbind(male, admitted) ~ engineering, data = berk_sub, FUN = mean)
#>   engineering      male  admitted
#> 1           0 0.3270548 0.2517123
#> 2           1 0.9572650 0.6324786

# Odds ratios for men, before and after controlling for department
fit0 <- glm(admitted ~ male, data = berk_sub, family = binomial)
fit1 <- glm(admitted ~ male + engineering, data = berk_sub,
            family = binomial)
exp(coef(fit0))
#> (Intercept)        male 
#>   0.3615635   3.2547852 
exp(coef(fit1))
#> (Intercept)        male engineering 
#>   0.3227341   1.1321297   4.7354328
